For this lab, I made a small Flutter app where one card responds to three gestures. A tap changes the card’s color, adds to the tap count, and shows a confirmation message. A horizontal swipe changes the card’s color too, but also records whether the movement went left or right. Holding the card for a moment resets the counts and restores its starting appearance. Keeping the actions on one card gave me a simple way to compare how different gestures can produce different results in the same interface.

Each gesture serves a slightly different purpose. Tapping is a quick way to make a change, and the count lets the user see that the tap registered. Swiping makes the card feel more interactive and gives the user direction-specific feedback. The long press provides a reset without adding another button to the screen. The changing color and status text help communicate the result, while haptic feedback gives a brief physical confirmation on supported devices.

One challenge was separating a regular tap from a long press, since both start with a finger touching the screen. I used Flutter’s tap and long-press callbacks so each action has its own response. I also handled horizontal dragging to distinguish left and right swipes. While testing, I noticed the interaction should work across the whole card, not only over a small icon. I made the card the active gesture area and added an animated color transition, counts, and haptic feedback to make the response easier to notice.

I tested the app on an Android emulator by tapping the card, swiping in both directions, and holding it to reset. The text, counts, color, and reset behavior responded as expected. The screenshots document the starting screen and the visible outcomes. This exercise helped me see how stateful widgets can update the interface after input, while gesture callbacks connect a person’s actions to specific changes. The app is intentionally small, but the same approach could support richer controls in a larger app.
